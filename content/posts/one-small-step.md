title: "One Small Step, One Giant Leap for Tonderai"

description: "On slow starts, the cost of weekends, and the spirit that keeps asking for more — a reflection on small leaps taken one after the other."
---

The world has always felt too large for me, or maybe I have always been too small for it.

I started my journey in software engineering at a startup in 2021. I had just resigned from my job as a mechatronics engineer. I resigned because I felt out of place; the work simply did not interest me enough, and I found myself spending my spare time learning about software development. So when I was offered a software developer internship, I grabbed it with both arms.

That was the first of the small leaps that brought me to where I am now, and the operative word is *small*. I had applied to other software companies and received no offers. Each rejection convinced me a little more that I wasn't good enough for a full role. An internship was the door that opened, and honestly, it felt like the only one I deserved.

I can still recall my first task. I had to build an Android application to test our BLE (Bluetooth Low Energy) SDK. The only problem was that I had never built an Android app. I had heard of Android Studio in college, but at the time I convinced myself to focus on calculus. My team lead walked me through the SDK on a call. He shared his screen and opened IntelliJ. The SDK was written in Kotlin, a language I didn't know, and it was beautifully architected: modular, built with expansion and scale in mind. That also made it difficult to read. In that first call we covered dependency injection, some of the SOLID principles and other programming paradigms. I remember thinking, *why did I choose to do this?* I could have been a mechatronics engineer, doing something I was comfortable with.

Over the next few weeks my code quality was poor and I was slow. Painfully slow. It was the best I could do at the time. I started working every weekend, and it became part of my routine. I wasn't doing it to get ahead; I was doing it to stay afloat. My teammates had some experience building software, while I was leaning on what I had picked up doing IT in high school. I questioned myself a lot. I questioned my degree, and I wished I had studied computer science instead. The weekends bothered me most, because I only worked them so I wouldn't fall too far behind, so I would have some progress to share with my peers. In a way they felt like evidence that I was not good enough. Even with the extra hours, I wasn't fast enough, and my code still wasn't up to standard.

I was extended a lot of grace. My team kept challenging me even as I failed, when they could just as easily have given up on me. Still, I retreated further into my shell as the months went by. I became more competent, but I was never as sharp as my teammates. I didn't always feel the comparisons were fair given my background, so to protect myself, I made myself small. I only volunteered for tasks I knew I could accomplish, tasks that wouldn't expose me, tasks I could wrap up on a Sunday afternoon so I'd have something to report at Monday's standup.

That lasted a while, until I started to feel the way I had in my engineering job. I had stopped growing. Eventually I found the courage to tell my manager that I didn't want to be just the Bluetooth guy. I wanted to work on the backend. He listened, and he obliged, with a task far beyond anything I was prepared for. That was the price of my bravery.

This leap was even more uncomfortable, but there was also excitement. I felt I was finally being accepted by my peers and trusted with something important. I was now working on a Spring Boot backend with about six microservices. If you don't know what that means, don't feel bad; I didn't either.

Every new feature felt like a test, a test on things I didn't know yet. Each pull request confronted me with new concepts: principles I had never heard of, patterns everyone else seemed to take for granted. The comments were always framed as *you did this wrong because it violates that principle*, and it was often my first time hearing of the principle at all. It got overwhelming enough that I started meditating.

I felt exposed, but I was intrigued at the same time. Sometimes the comments on a pull request led to completely different code. Still, I refused to resolve a comment until I understood why. If you told me I couldn't do something, my answer was: well, it works on my machine, so please explain what I'm missing. I would ask for meetings just to go through the comments. Brazen? Maybe. But it forced my peers to explain things in a way I could understand, and I made sure to carry that into every new pull request. I tried to make only new mistakes, and with each one I learnt a new concept. It felt as though software engineering revealed itself to me the more poorly I did it. That was encouraging, and also somewhat soul-crushing, because where does it end?

The pressure to finish was exhausting, and once more the weekends were sacrificed. That made my progress reports feel inauthentic. I was too ashamed to say how hard I'd had to work to get there. The tasks weren't objectively difficult, but for me they were, and admitting that felt like admitting I didn't belong.

Even conversations with my peers about software engineering became hard. I would mentally check out. Those conversations felt like a material representation of my inadequacy, and it was easier to sit back and not listen. Yet I yearned to be involved, to contribute. I just felt I had no footing.

I got the job done eventually, with lots of bumps along the way, and my speed was pointed out to me again. How could I feel proud of something I had done too slowly?

I eventually moved on from that job, but a lot of it stayed with me. I kept doubting myself. I found it hard to acknowledge my own successes, because even then a voice in my head would tell me I had been too slow, or that mine wasn't the best solution.

Then I got comfortable again, and once more I wanted more. Something in me refused to accept that this was it. So I moved into cloud engineering and serverless systems. This was another job with a lot of unknowns, and the learning gradient was steep. I wanted help, someone to hold on to as I slid down trying to make sense of things. The help didn't come the way I had envisioned, but I learnt a lot, and I got back on my feet.

I'm grateful for the ways my peers challenged me, because at times they did what my own will wouldn't. They held me to the same standards they held each other to. In a way, that was the acceptance I had been yearning for. They didn't lower the bar because I didn't come from a traditional software engineering background. It was tough, but I would never have dared myself to learn about SOLID or software architecture otherwise. There was no capacity to give me small tasks just so I could learn. We had to deliver. Keeping the bar high, and trusting that I could reach it, was its own kind of grace.

I'm slowly starting to realise that I can't punish myself in retrospect for not knowing what I didn't know. I always tried my best. But there were gaps in my understanding, and when you're delivering a product to clients, my best wasn't always good enough. Both of those things can be true at the same time.

The irony is that nowadays, once I get comfortable, I'm ridiculously fast, sometimes to the point of burnout. I think part of me is still trying to prove I'm good enough. The difference is that it's mostly just Sundays now. Saturdays, I read.

Looking back, every leap I took was small, even though each one took every fibre of my will.

And now I find myself taking another one. I'll be starting a new role building agentic solutions. This leap feels large, maybe because I'm looking at it from the front, with all of it still ahead of me. But I only got here through small leaps, one after the other. Will I be slow? Probably. Do I know it will work out? No, but I have to find out. Am I good enough? I don't know, but I'm willing to swing the bat, even if I strike out.

I don't know what it is in me that keeps asking for more, and then dares me to go and ask for it. But it has led me into rooms I didn't think I belonged in, and I'm grateful for it. That is the spirit I'll lean on for what comes next.

I'm proud of the little boy in me who believes he can take on this next challenge. I'm proud of the parts of me that are scared but willing to try anyway. And I'm proud of myself for not going back into my shell.
